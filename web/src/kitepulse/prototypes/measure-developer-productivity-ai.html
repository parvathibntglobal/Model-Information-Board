How to measure developer productivity with AI

By the Kite Pulse teamUpdated 22 October 202611 min read

Measuring developer productivity with AI means comparing how fast and how well work reaches users, with AI and without it, against a fair baseline. Counting what the AI produced tells you very little. Commits, lines of code and accepted suggestions all rise the moment a team adopts AI, whether or not delivery improves.

Use delivery and quality metrics, a comparison group, and a look at the process inside AI sessions, where the time now goes.

AI coding assistant productivity impact: guess first

Is AI increasing developer productivity? Before you read any study, say what you expect. How much faster do AI coding assistants make an experienced developer on real work? Set your guess, then compare it with what researchers predicted and what they measured.

  • GitHub Copilot lab task, 2023. Developers asked to write an HTTP server in JavaScript "completed the task 55.8% faster" with Copilot. One small task, written from scratch.
  • Google enterprise trial, 2024. 96 Google engineers on an enterprise task. The authors' estimate of time saved "stands at about 21%, although our confidence interval is large".
  • METR, early 2025. 16 experienced developers on 246 real issues in large open-source repositories they knew well. AI "increases completion time by 19%". Before starting, developers forecast 24% less time; afterwards they estimated 20% less. Economists predicted 39% and ML experts 38%.
  • METR follow-up, late 2025 agents. "Small (~4-20%) productivity benefits", which METR expects to be "an underestimate due to selection effects": developers increasingly declined to work without AI.

Each study words its result differently: time saved on a task, or a productivity benefit. The chart places each figure as its source states it, on one scale, so that the gap between belief and measurement is visible. It is not a single comparable measure.

Why are we shipping more commits but not delivering faster?

Commits measure activity. Delivery depends on everything after the commit: review, testing, release, and fixing what broke. When AI makes writing code cheaper but the work queues at review or comes back as rework, commit counts climb and lead time does not move.

The pattern also shows up at industry scale. Google's 2024 DORA report found that "as AI adoption increased, it was accompanied by an estimated decrease in delivery throughput by 1.5%, and an estimated reduction in delivery stability by 7.2%". That is a statistical association across survey respondents, not proof about your team. It is a reason to measure delivery itself rather than the activity that feeds it.

How to measure developer productivity: four areas to cover

No single number answers whether AI is helping. Cover four areas at team level: adoption (who uses AI, and for what), delivery (is work reaching users faster), quality (is it holding up) and experience (how the work feels, and where it gets stuck). Adoption is the input. The other three show the result.

05

Design a fair test of AI on your team

You can find out whether AI coding tools help your team complete work faster, but only by comparing like with like. The strongest simple design is a staggered rollout: half the team starts now, half starts later, and you compare the two groups over the same weeks. A before-and-after comparison is weaker, because anything else that changed in those weeks looks like an AI effect.

What AI sessions show that commits cannot

      
When AI writes most of the code, the commit is the last line of a longer story. Inside the session are the questions asked, the fixes the AI retried, and the approaches that were tried and dropped. That is where a team finds out why a task took 40 minutes instead of 10, and what to fix so the next one does not.

    Why token usage leaderboards for engineers backfire
    
    
    
      
    Tokenmaxxing is using as many AI tokens as possible, usually because token use is tracked or ranked as a sign of productivity. A leaderboard of tokens per engineer turns a cost into a target. Spend goes up whether or not more useful work gets done, and the people who rank highest may simply have the longest-running agents.
    
      
    Tokens belong in two places: budgets, where they explain what AI costs, and session analysis, where a spike can point at a loop the AI could not get out of. In both, the question is what happened to the work, not who used the most. For the cost side, see how to track AI coding costs across developers, teams and projects. For the people side, see how to track AI usage ethically.

    Measuring developer productivity with AI: questions
    
    
    
      
    Is using AI coding assistants actually making developers more productive?
    It depends on the work. Controlled studies found large speed-ups on set tasks: 55.8% faster on one small JavaScript task, and about 21% in an enterprise trial at Google. In METR's 2025 study of experienced developers working on their own mature repositories, tasks took 19% longer with AI, and METR's late-2025 follow-up found small benefits of about 4 to 20% that it expects are an underestimate. The reliable answer for your team is your own measurement against a baseline.
    
      
    Can generative AI improve developer productivity?
    Yes, in some settings. In the published studies, speed-ups were largest on small, self-contained tasks, and absent in METR's 2025 study of experienced developers working in large codebases they already knew well. Whether it improves productivity on your team depends on the kind of work, the tools and how they are used, so measure delivery and quality before and after rollout.
    
      
    How do I measure software developer productivity?
    Measure four areas at team level: adoption (who uses AI and for what), delivery (lead time and deployment frequency), quality (change failure rate and rework) and experience (where work gets stuck). Compare each against a baseline. Never rank individuals by output counts such as commits or lines of code.
    
      
    How do I measure developer productivity when AI writes most of the code?
    Stop counting what was produced and measure what reached users and how it held up: lead time for changes, deployment frequency, change failure rate and rework. Add a view of the process inside AI sessions, such as retries of the same fix and approaches that were dropped, because that is where time now goes.
    
      
    Are commits and pull requests still good productivity metrics with AI?
    Not on their own. AI makes code cheap to produce, so commit and pull request counts rise as soon as a team adopts it, whether or not delivery improves. Use them only as context next to lead time and change failure rate.
    
      
    Why are we shipping more commits but not delivering faster?
    Commits measure activity, and delivery depends on everything after the commit: review, testing, release and fixing what broke. If AI speeds up writing code but the work queues at review or comes back as rework, commits rise and lead time stays flat. Measure lead time and rework to find where the time goes.
    
      
    How do I improve developer productivity with AI?
    Start where AI sessions go wrong. Repeated failures, retries of the same fix and abandoned approaches usually point to a broken test setup, missing project context or a task that is too large. Fix those causes once, then check the effect against your baseline with one delivery metric and one quality metric.
    
      
    Does higher AI usage correlate with higher developer productivity?
    Not reliably. Usage rises with adoption whether or not outcomes improve, and the heaviest users may simply have the largest tasks. Google's 2024 DORA report found that as AI adoption increased, it was accompanied by an estimated 1.5% decrease in delivery throughput and a 7.2% reduction in delivery stability. Compare outcomes against a baseline instead of correlating usage with output.
    
      
    Can I determine whether AI coding tools help developers complete tasks faster?
    Yes, with a fair test. Roll AI out to half the team first, or compare against a similar team, keep a baseline of at least one release cycle, and compare lead time and a quality metric over the same weeks. A simple before-and-after comparison is weak evidence, because anything else that changed in those weeks looks like an AI effect.
    
      
    How do I benchmark my team's AI adoption against other teams?
    Benchmark against your own baseline first. Figures from other companies mix different codebases, tools and metric definitions, so a gap may only mean the definitions differ. When comparing teams inside your company, use the same definitions, compare outcomes such as lead time rather than usage, and compare teams, never individuals.
    
      
    Are AI-generated changes increasing code review time?
    They can, because more code reaches review. Measure it from your git host: time from a pull request opening to its first review and to merge, plus pull request size, compared with your baseline. Git-based engineering analytics tools report this directly.
    
      
    Is AI usage affecting the quality of our code?
    Look at outcomes: change failure rate, rework soon after merge, escaped bugs and time to restore service, each against your baseline. Google's 2024 DORA report associated rising AI adoption with an estimated 7.2% reduction in delivery stability, so quality is worth tracking from the first week of a rollout.
    
      
    Are developers spending less time on repetitive coding tasks?
    Find out by classifying the work first: label what time goes to bug fixes, new features, tests and boilerplate, then compare the mix before and after rollout. Without that split, a drop in repetitive work and a drop in all work look the same.
    
      
    What is tokenmaxxing?
    Tokenmaxxing is using as many AI tokens as possible, usually because token use is tracked or ranked as a sign of productivity. It turns a cost into a target: spend rises whether or not more useful work gets done. Track tokens to manage budgets, not to judge people.
