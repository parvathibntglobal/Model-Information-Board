Why Claude Code team usage limits belong in sprint planning

Claude Code reports plan usage per account against a 5-hour window, a weekly window and a weekly window for each model. That makes a team's capacity for AI-heavy work uneven in a way story points never show. Two developers with the same velocity can sit on very different amounts of remaining plan: one has spent the week on a long refactor with an agent, the other on reviews and meetings.

The cost of ignoring this is familiar to anyone who has watched it happen. A large migration lands on the person who has already used most of their week. On day three they hit the limit, the agent stops, and the task either waits for the window to reset or moves to someone who has to pick up the context cold. Neither shows up in the sprint plan, because the plan never knew.

Treating Claude Code team usage limits as one more planning input, like leave or on-call, fixes most of that. It does not need a forecast. It needs a short check of a few numbers before tasks are handed out.

The three inputs to check before you assign heavy AI work

Plan pressure

How much of each window is used

The weekly window decides who can take a multi-day agent task. The 5-hour window decides who can start one this afternoon. The per-model weekly window matters when the task needs a specific model.

Availability

Leave, holidays and on-call

Someone on leave for half the sprint has half the time, whatever their plan reading. Someone on call has interruptions that break long agent sessions.

Recent activity

What each person has been doing

A high reading after a week of heavy agent work tells you something different from a high reading built in one afternoon. Recent token use and active time give the reading its context.

None of the three is enough alone. A low plan reading on someone who starts leave on Wednesday is not headroom you can use. A high reading on someone whose weekly window resets tomorrow may be fine for a task that starts next week.

A planning routine, step by step

This takes a few minutes before the planning meeting and a quick look again halfway through the sprint.

  1. List the AI-heavy tasks. Mark the tickets you expect to run as long agent sessions: migrations, large refactors, test generation across a codebase, anything where the developer will hand the agent a big brief and let it work.

  2. Read each person's weekly window. Sort the team from most used to least. Anyone at 80% or more is near the limit; anyone at 100% is at it.

  3. Check the per-model window for the model the task needs. A person can have room in the overall weekly window and still be close to the limit for one model.

  4. Take out leave and on-call. Remove days off from each person's capacity, and treat on-call days as unsuitable for starting a long agent run.

  5. Look at recent activity. Check token use per day over the last week or two. A steady pattern makes the reading a fair guide to next week; a single spike may not repeat.

  6. Assign and time the large tasks. Give them to people with room, and schedule them early in a fresh window rather than at the end of a used one. Move a large task to the start of next week if nobody has room this week.

  7. Recheck at mid-sprint. Usage moves faster than a plan. If someone is now near a limit, swap their remaining heavy task for review work or smaller fixes.

How to predict Claude Code usage without a forecast

You rarely need a model of future usage. You need to know whether next week is likely to look like the last few, and that comes from history. Three things help.

  • Weekly peaks over time. If a person's weekly window has peaked above 80% in most recent weeks, assume the next heavy task will push them to the limit.
  • Windows at the limit. Count how often a person has hit the 5-hour or weekly limit. Repeated 5-hour limits suggest long single sessions; weekly limits suggest sustained load.
  • The shape of the work. Tasks that lean on sub-agents, background commands or plan mode can use far more than quick chat-style help. The tasks you marked in step one are the ones to weigh.

This is judgement on top of counts, and it should stay that way. A plan reading says how much of a window is used. It does not say whether that use was worthwhile, and it should not be read as a measure of how hard anyone works.

A team availability dashboard from what you already record

Most teams already keep leave in an HR system and on-call in a rota tool. The trouble is that neither sits next to the plan readings, so nobody looks at them together. Putting the three on one screen is what turns a plan reading into a planning input.

What Claude Code team usage visibility covers, and what it does not

What you can see

  • Claude plan readings from Claude Code: 5-hour, weekly and per-model weekly windows
  • Who is near (80% or more) or at (100%) a limit, worst first
  • Recorded leave, holidays and on-call rotas
  • Token use per person, model and day

What you cannot

  • Plan limits for Codex, Cursor, Gemini CLI or other tools: they show "Not reported by this tool"
  • A forecast of next week's usage
  • Readings fresher than Claude Code's last save, unless the background refresh is on
  • Who should take a task: that is still your call

If your team does most of its agent work in a tool other than Claude Code, plan readings will not help you plan that work, and you will need that vendor's own admin view. For the limits themselves and how the windows behave, see our guide to Claude Code usage limits.

Questions about planning around Claude limits

How do I plan sprints around AI plan limits?

Before the planning meeting, check each person's weekly Claude window and the per-model window for the model the big tasks need, then take out anyone on leave or on call. Give the large, AI-heavy tasks to people with room in their weekly window, and schedule them early in that window rather than at its end. Recheck halfway through the sprint, because usage moves faster than a plan.

How do I account for leave and on-call when measuring AI usage?

Record them, so a quiet week is read as a week off and not as a change in how someone works. In Kite Pulse, Leave & On-Call holds leave, holidays and duty rotas so insights do not misread them; managers edit their own reporting line and company holidays are admin only. When planning, treat someone on call as having less room for long AI sessions, whatever their plan reading says.

How can I tell if a developer is about to hit their Claude usage limit?

Look at their 5-hour and weekly window readings from Claude Code. In Kite Pulse, the Plan headroom tab on Team AI Usage lists people worst first and marks a window as near at 80% or more and at limit at 100%, with old readings flagged as stale. It covers Claude Code only, and a reading is only as fresh as the last time Claude Code saved it unless an admin switches on the background refresh.
