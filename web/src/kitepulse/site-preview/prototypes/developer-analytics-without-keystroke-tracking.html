Why developers fight keystroke and screen tools

Most engineering leaders who have tried a keystroke counter, a screen recorder or an activity tracker have a story about how it went. The tool was installed, the dashboards filled up, and within weeks the team was arguing about the tool instead of the work. The objections are usually sharper than "we don't like being watched", and they are worth taking seriously because they predict what happens next.

  • They record the person, not the work. A keystroke count or a stream of screenshots says someone was at a desk. It says nothing about whether a bug was fixed or a design got better.
  • They measure the wrong activity now. When an AI assistant writes much of the code, typing volume tells you very little. A developer who spends an hour reviewing and steering an agent may type less than one who pastes the same error ten times.
  • They collect far more than anyone needs. Screens show private messages, banking tabs and medical bookings. Nobody asked for that data, and everyone knows it is sitting somewhere.
  • Nobody knows who reads it. If the answer to "who can see my screen history?" is vague, people assume the worst.

What a failed rollout leaves behind

The visible cost of a resented tool is morale. The less visible cost is the data. People who distrust a collector find ways around it: they move side work to a personal laptop, keep the tracked machine busy, or simply stop doing exploratory work where it is recorded. Each workaround is reasonable from where the developer sits, and each one leaves a gap.

The result is a dashboard that looks complete and is not. Busy machines read as productive people, quiet ones read as idle, and the experiments that matter most never appear. A leader making staffing or tooling decisions from that picture is worse off than one with no dashboard, because the gaps are invisible.

So the question to ask of any replacement is less "what can it see?" and more "will people leave it running with nothing to hide from?"

What developers tend to accept instead

The tools that survive contact with an engineering team share a few properties. None of them is about clever technology; they are about who decides and who can check.

Track token usage without collecting prompts

Many teams start with one narrow question: how much AI are we using, and on which tools? That question does not need anyone's conversation. Token counts answer it on their own.

A counts-only setting is the strongest signal you can send a sceptical team. It says the organisation wants volume and adoption, and has chosen not to hold the text. You can widen collection later, project by project, once people have seen what the view is used for. Starting wide and narrowing after complaints rarely recovers the trust.

Pseudonymised developer analytics: what it can and cannot mean

"Pseudonymised" is a word worth pinning down before it goes into an announcement. In most team analytics, people are named on their own team's pages, because a lead needs to know who is stuck in order to help. What can be pseudonymised is the material around them: the personal email address an AI tool is signed in with, or the full folder paths on someone's laptop.

Be precise in what you promise. If developers are told the data is anonymous and then find their name on a team page, the rollout is over. Tell them which fields are hashed or hidden, and which pages show their name, and to whom.

Developer analytics without source code access

The other worry is code. A tool that reads AI session records does not need to open a repository's files to show usage. The honest caveat is that a session record is a conversation with an assistant that read and wrote code, so excerpts of code can appear in it. Secrets in those excerpts should be masked before they leave the machine.

Rolling it out so it is not read as surveillance

No product removes the need to explain yourself. A few steps make the difference between a team that shrugs and a team that searches for an off switch.

  1. Say what is collected before installing anything. List what is read, what is not, and where it is stored. Show the privacy page of the tool you picked rather than paraphrasing it.

  2. Say what it is for, and what it is not for. If it informs AI budgets and tool choices, say so. If it will not feed performance reviews, write that down and keep to it.

  3. Name who can see what. In Kite Pulse a manager sees their own reporting line, enforced by the server, and estimated cost per developer is admin-only. Some admin views list the most active people, so agree in advance how those lists will be used.

  4. Start narrow. Counts only, or a few volunteer projects, then widen once people have seen the pages.

  5. Give developers something back. Their own sessions, work patterns and plan usage are useful to them, and a private view of their own data is the clearest proof that the tool is not only for managers.

Questions about tracking AI use without surveillance

How do I track AI coding usage without monitoring keystrokes?

Read the records the AI coding tools already keep. Each assistant saves its own session history on the developer's computer, with the conversation, tool calls and token counts. Analytics built on those records shows which tools are used, on which projects and at what volume, without anything watching the keyboard or the screen.

Will developers see AI usage tracking as surveillance?

Some will, whatever tool you choose, if they learn about it after it is installed. Resistance drops when people are told what is collected before anything is sent, can see their own data, and know exactly who else can see it. Collecting less where you can, such as token counts only for some tools, also helps.

Does Kite Pulse read my source code?

No. It reads the AI tools' own session records, folder names and repository addresses, and it does not open a project's source files. Session records can include excerpts of code the AI read or wrote, with secrets masked. Optional reads of package manifests and project notes are off until an admin turns them on.

Can my manager see everything I type to the AI?

Kite Pulse does not record keystrokes, so it never sees typing that was not sent to the AI tool. What it reads is the session record the tool saved, which includes your conversation. Managers see the same report you see for people in their own reporting line, without your private prompt statistics, and your Privacy page shows what is collected about you and who can see each part. An admin can also set a tool to token counts only, so no conversation is uploaded for it.
