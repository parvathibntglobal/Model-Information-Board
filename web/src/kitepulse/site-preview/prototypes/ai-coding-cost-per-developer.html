How to Calculate AI Coding Costs per Developer, and How the Estimate Works

Kite Pulse teamUpdated 2 Nov 20268 min read

Here is how to calculate AI coding costs per developer: for each tool, multiply each person's tokens by the list price of every model they used, then add the flat subscriptions and seat prices you pay for them. Keep the parts apart rather than adding them into one figure, because a subscription costs the same however much it is used, while a usage estimate only says what the tokens would cost at list price. The result is an estimate that explains your invoices; it does not replace them.

The three parts of an AI coding bill

The cost of AI coding tools reaches a company in three shapes, and each one is calculated differently. Most teams pay in at least two of them at once.

Usage

Tokens times price

Usage-billed tools charge per token, the unit of text sent to the model and received back. Cost is tokens multiplied by the price for that model, usually with a different price for input and output.

Flat

Subscriptions

A monthly plan per person. The amount is fixed whether the developer runs one session or a hundred, though heavy use can hit the plan's limits.

Seats

Seat prices

A per-seat licence bought by the company. Like a subscription it is flat, and its cost belongs to whoever holds the seat, used or not.

So the per-developer calculation has two halves. The usage half is arithmetic on token counts. The flat half is a lookup: which plans and seats does this person hold, and what do they cost. Our comparison of AI coding assistant prices and plans is a starting point for the second half.

Why cost per developer should stay per tool

Once you have figures for several tools, the temptation is to add them into one number per person. That number mixes three kinds of figure: a list-price estimate, a cost a tool reported about itself, and a flat fee. It also hides the tools you could not price at all. A gap added in as zero makes a tool look free, and a total built that way looks precise when it is not.

Keeping cost per tool avoids both problems. The reader sees which figures are estimates, which are reported, and which tools have no price, and can decide what to add up for their own purpose. When finance needs one company total, the invoice total is the right figure; the per-tool view explains how it splits.

Tools that report their own cost: Aider, Cline and OpenCode

Some AI coding tools work out a cost for each session themselves and store it in their own history. Aider, Cline, Roo Code, Kilo Code and OpenCode all do. That makes their cost easier to collect, and it changes what the number means: it is the tool's own calculation, made with whatever prices the tool knows for the model it called.

Aider AI cost is recorded in Aider's history alongside its tokens and model, so the per-session figure is already there to read; see the Aider integration for what else is collected. Cline cost works the same way, and Roo Code and Kilo Code match it; the Cline integration page covers setup. OpenCode cost is also the tool's own figure, and OpenCode is the one of these tools that shows an account; details are on the OpenCode integration page.

For a per-developer calculation, treat these figures as reported rather than estimated by you. They are useful and quick, but they sit in their own column next to any list-price estimate you make for other tools. How far to trust a cost estimate

An estimate built this way is honest about its inputs, and it still differs from the invoice for predictable reasons. Say them up front and the gap stops being a surprise.

  • Flat plans. A developer on a subscription pays the same at any usage. Their list-price estimate shows how heavily they use the tool, which helps when choosing a plan, but it is not money spent.
  • Contract terms. List prices leave out discounts, credits and negotiated rates, so a company on a contract usually pays something different.
  • Reported figures. A tool's own cost is only as good as that tool's price table for the model it called.
  • Unpriced tools. Usage on a tool with no known price shows up as tokens without a dollar figure. Count those tokens when you compare, or the tool will look cheaper than it is.

None of these make the estimate useless. Tokens per developer, tool and model are measured, and they show where usage goes, which is the question an invoice cannot answer. For presenting these figures to finance, see the engineering AI spend report, and for the token side on its own, token usage per developer.

One last caution. A per-developer cost describes how AI was used; it says nothing on its own about the value of the work. Read it with the model, the project and the kind of task before drawing a conclusion about a person.

Questions about AI coding costs per developer

How accurate are AI coding cost estimates?

The token counts can be exact, because they come from each tool's own records. The dollar figure is only as accurate as the price applied to them: list prices ignore discounts and contract terms, and a developer on a flat subscription pays the same whatever they use. Treat an estimate as a guide to where usage goes and keep the vendor invoice as the record of what was paid.

Are Kite Pulse cost figures exact?

No, and they are labelled that way. Kite Pulse multiplies tokens by the vendor's published list price for Claude and Gemini models, uses the cost Aider, Cline, Roo Code, Kilo Code and OpenCode report themselves, and shows a dash where no price is known. Every figure is marked "not billed spend" and is meant to sit beside your invoices, not replace them.

Which AI coding tools are costing us the most?

Your invoices answer that for money actually paid. For usage, compare tools side by side rather than as one total: estimated cost for the priced tools, token counts for the rest, and seat prices beside both. Kite Pulse shows cost per tool on Team AI Usage and never adds it up across tools, because a list-price estimate, a tool-reported figure and a tool with no price are not the same kind of number.
